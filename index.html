1. Preference and Utility

Utility is a numerical representation of preferences. A utility function assigns a number to every consumption bundle such that more-preferred bundles get larger numbers.

Key Concepts

  • Total utility: total satisfaction from consuming a bundle
  • Marginal utility (MU): extra satisfaction from one additional unit
  • Monotonicity: more of a good is always preferred
  • Diminishing marginal utility: as you consume more, each extra unit gives less additional satisfaction
MUx = ∂U / ∂x Marginal utility of good x
Example: First slice of pizza → 40 utility. Second → 25. Third → 10. Diminishing marginal utility.

2. Indifference Curves

An indifference curve shows all bundles that give the consumer the same utility. Along the curve, the consumer is indifferent.

Four Properties

  1. Higher curves preferred: more utility
  2. Downward sloping: give up one good → need more of the other
  3. Never cross: would violate property 1
  4. Bowed inward (convex): diminishing MRS

Special Cases

3. Marginal Rate of Substitution

The MRS is the rate at which a consumer will trade one good for another while staying on the same indifference curve.

MRS = − Δx₂ / Δx₁ = MU₁ / MU₂ Slope of the indifference curve (absolute value)
Diminishing MRS: As you get more of good 1, you give up less of good 2 for another unit. This is why indifference curves are convex.

Why Diminishing MRS Happens

  • More x₁ → MU₁ falls
  • Less x₂ → MU₂ rises
  • So MRS = MU₁/MU₂ falls

4. Consumer Optimization

Consumer maximizes utility subject to a budget constraint.

max U(C₁, C₂)   s.t.   P₁C₁ + P₂C₂ ≤ W

Optimality Condition

MU₁ / P₁ = MU₂ / P₂ Equal marginal utility per dollar

Equivalently:

MRS = P₁ / P₂ Indifference curve tangent to budget line

Intuition

If MU₁/P₁ > MU₂/P₂, the last dollar on good 1 gives more utility. Buy more of good 1 until equal.

Dollar-by-Dollar Allocation

  1. Compute MU per dollar: MU/P
  2. Spend each dollar on the good with the highest MU/P
  3. Stop when budget exhausted or MU/P equalizes
Linda's example: Handbags $1, Trips $2, Income $9.
MU/$ for 1st bag = 16, 1st trip = 14, 2nd bag = 12, 2nd trip = 11, 3rd bag = 9, 3rd trip = 9.
Optimal: 3 bags + 3 trips → total utility = 37 + 68 = 105

5. Income vs Substitution Effects

When a price changes, the total change in quantity demanded decomposes into two effects.

Substitution Effect

The good becomes relatively cheaper → consumers substitute toward it.

Always positive

Income Effect

The price change alters real purchasing power.

  • Normal good: positive
  • Inferior good: negative

Slutsky Identity

ΔC₁ = [C(P₁', W') − C(P₁, W)] + [C(P₁', W) − C(P₁', W')] Substitution effect + Income effect
Giffen Good: If income effect dominates substitution effect, a price decrease leads to a decrease in quantity demanded. Violates law of demand.

Real-World Evidence

Jensen & Miller (2008): 1% decrease in rice prices → 0.24% decrease in rice consumption in poor Chinese households. Rice was a staple; when cheaper, households bought meat instead.

6. Law of Demand

Law of Demand: Quantity demanded falls when price rises, other things constant.

Follows from optimization: if a good is normal, substitution effect (negative) dominates or reinforces income effect.

9. Elasticity Along a Demand Curve

For a linear demand curve, elasticity changes along the curve even though slope is constant.

ε = (∂Q/∂P) · (P/Q) Slope constant; P/Q changes
LocationPQP/Q|ε|
Top left (price axis)HighLowLargeElastic
MidpointMediumMedium1Unit elastic
Bottom right (quantity axis)LowHighSmallInelastic
Intuition: Near the top, a $1 price change is a small % of a high price, but the quantity change is a large % of a small quantity. Near the bottom, the opposite.
Caveat: This pattern holds for linear demand. A constant-elasticity demand curve (Q = AP−α) has the same elasticity everywhere.

11. Marginal Cost vs Average Total Cost

  • When MC < ATC, ATC is falling
  • When MC > ATC, ATC is rising
  • MC crosses ATC at its minimum

Mathematical Proof

Let ATC = TC/Q. Differentiate:

d(ATC)/dQ = (MC · Q − TC) / Q² = (MC − ATC) / Q

Since Q > 0:

  • MC < ATC → derivative negative → ATC falling
  • MC > ATC → derivative positive → ATC rising
  • MC = ATC → derivative zero → ATC at minimum
Analogy: If your exam average is 80% and you score 70% on the next test, average falls. If you score 90%, average rises. If exactly 80%, average stays at its turning point.

12. Law of Supply

Law of Supply: Quantity supplied rises as price rises, other things constant.

Follows from profit maximization:

max π = P(q)·q − C(q) − F

For a price-taking firm, supply function:

P = MC(q) Firm produces where price equals marginal cost

15. Perfect Competition

Assumptions

  1. Homogeneous product
  2. No taxes or transaction costs; factors freely mobile
  3. Market outcomes affect only buyers and sellers in that market
  4. Participants infinitely small → price takers
  5. Buyers and sellers fully informed
  6. All individuals self-interested
  7. All possible scenarios fully accounted for

16. Consumer and Producer Surplus

Consumer Surplus

WTP: Maximum a buyer will pay

CS = WTP − P

Area below demand, above price.

Producer Surplus

WTS: Minimum a seller will accept (= cost)

PS = P − cost

Area above supply, below price.

Total Surplus and Efficiency

TS = CS + PS

Efficiency: Allocation maximizes total surplus.

Free market outcomes:
  • Allocate supply to buyers who value it most
  • Allocate demand to sellers who produce at lowest cost
  • Produce quantity that maximizes CS + PS

17. Deadweight Loss

DWL is the fall in total surplus from a market distortion (tax, price control, monopoly).

DWL = ½ × tax × (Qno tax − Qwith tax) Area of the triangle of lost trades

Why DWL Occurs

A tax drives a wedge between buyer price and seller price. Some mutually beneficial trades no longer happen. Their surplus is destroyed — nobody gets it.

What Determines DWL Size

FactorEffect on DWL
Tax rate (t)Larger t → larger DWL (quadratically)
Demand elasticityMore elastic → larger DWL
Supply elasticityMore elastic → larger DWL
Market sizeLarger market → larger absolute DWL
DWL ∝ t² Doubling the tax quadruples the deadweight loss

19. Laffer Curve

The Laffer curve shows the relationship between tax rate and tax revenue.

Two Forces at Play

  • Price effect: Higher tax per unit → more revenue per unit
  • Quantity effect: Higher tax → fewer units sold → less revenue

At low taxes, price effect dominates (revenue rises). At high taxes, quantity effect dominates (revenue falls).

The Shape

Tax Revenue = t × Q(t) Revenue is zero at t = 0 and at t = prohibitive

Inverted U-shape: rises, peaks, then falls.

Where is the peak? Economists debate this. Most modern estimates suggest developed countries are on the left side of the curve for most taxes.

20. Price Controls

Price Ceiling

Legal maximum price, set below equilibrium.

Why: Make essentials affordable; prevent gouging.

Result: Shortages, black markets, reduced quality.

Examples: Rent control, anti-gouging laws.

Price Floor

Legal minimum price, set above equilibrium.

Why: Support incomes; protect producers.

Result: Surpluses, unemployment, wasted resources.

Examples: Minimum wage, agricultural supports.

Core Trade-off

GoalPolicyBenefitCost
Affordable goodsPrice ceilingLower pricesShortages
Support incomesPrice floorHigher incomesSurpluses
Bottom line: Price controls prevent markets from clearing. They create shortages (ceilings) or surpluses (floors).

21. Externalities

An externality occurs when a market outcome affects people who are not buyers or sellers in that market.

Negative Externality

Social cost > private cost.

Example: Pollution from a factory.

Result: Overproduction relative to social optimum.

Policy: Pigouvian tax, cap-and-trade.

Positive Externality

Social benefit > private benefit.

Example: Education, vaccination.

Result: Underproduction relative to social optimum.

Policy: Subsidy, public provision.

Coase Theorem

If property rights are well-defined and transaction costs are zero, parties can negotiate to internalize the externality regardless of initial assignment of rights.

In practice: Transaction costs, asymmetric information, and bargaining failures often prevent Coasean solutions. Government intervention may be needed.

22. Pure Exchange Economy

An economy where two parties have fixed endowments and trade to improve welfare. No production.

Setup

  • Party A has endowment (ωA¹, ωA²), utility uA(xA¹, xA²)
  • Party B has endowment (ωB¹, ωB²), utility uB(xB¹, xB²)
  • Prices p₁, p₂

The Edgeworth Box

The box shows the entire economy. Width = total Good 1, height = total Good 2.

Key Points

  • W: Endowment point (initial allocation)
  • M: Mutually beneficial trade
  • Blue lens: All trades that make both better off
  • Contract curve: All Pareto efficient allocations

Pareto Efficiency

An allocation is Pareto efficient if no one can be made better off without making someone worse off. This occurs where indifference curves are tangent:

MRSA = MRSB = p₁ / p₂

23. Comparative Advantage

Absolute Advantage

Produce more output with same inputs, or same output with fewer inputs.

Introduced by Adam Smith (1776).

Comparative Advantage

Produce at lower opportunity cost than another country.

Introduced by David Ricardo (1817).

Ricardo's Portugal-England Example

CountryCloth (hrs/unit)Wine (hrs/unit)
England1020
Portugal812

Portugal has absolute advantage in both goods.

Opportunity Costs

CountryOpp. cost of 1 ClothOpp. cost of 1 Wine
England0.5 Wine2 Cloth
Portugal0.67 Wine1.5 Cloth
Comparative advantage:
  • England: cloth (0.5 < 0.67)
  • Portugal: wine (1.5 < 2)

Autarky Relative Prices

PC / PW = aC / aW Relative price equals ratio of labor requirements
CountryPC/PW
England10/20 = 0.50
Portugal8/12 = 0.67

Why 1/a is productivity

  • aC = hours per unit of cloth (input measure)
  • 1/aC = units of cloth per hour (productivity)
  • Wage = price × productivity = P × (1/a)
  • Wages equalize across sectors → PC/PW = aC/aW

24. Terms of Trade

ToT = PEX / PIM Price of exports relative to imports

This is how many units of imports a country gets per unit of exports.

Why It Means "Units of Imports per Export"

If PEX = $10 and PIM = $5:

ToT = 10/5 = 2 1 export buys 2 imports

For England and Portugal

Key insight: Trade is positive-sum. Losers could in principle be compensated by winners. In practice, compensation rarely happens — this is the political economy of trade.

The "China Shock"

While trade raises total surplus, it creates concentrated losses in import-competing industries. These losses are politically salient and drive opposition to trade.
