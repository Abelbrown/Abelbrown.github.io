1 Preference and Utility

Utility is a numerical representation of preferences. A utility function assigns a number to every possible consumption bundle such that more-preferred bundles get larger numbers.

Total Utility (TU)

Total satisfaction from consuming a given quantity of a good.

Marginal Utility (MU)

Extra satisfaction from consuming one additional unit.

MUx = ∂U / ∂x Marginal utility of good x
Properties of Utility Functions
  • Monotonicity: more of a good is always preferred (higher utility)
  • Diminishing marginal utility: as you consume more, each extra unit gives less additional satisfaction
Example
First slice of pizza → 40 utility. Second slice → 25 utility. Third slice → 10 utility. This is diminishing marginal utility.

2 Indifference Curves

An indifference curve shows all combinations of two goods that give the consumer the same level of utility. Along the curve, the consumer is indifferent among all bundles.

Four Properties

  1. Higher curves preferred: Curves farther from the origin represent more utility
  2. Downward sloping: To keep utility constant, giving up one good requires more of the other
  3. Never cross: Crossing would imply the same bundle has two utility levels
  4. Bowed inward (convex): Reflects diminishing MRS

3 Marginal Rate of Substitution

The MRS is the rate at which a consumer is willing to trade one good for another while staying on the same indifference curve.

MRS = − Δx₂ / Δx₁ = MU₁ / MU₂ Slope of the indifference curve (absolute value)
Diminishing MRS: As you get more of good 1, you're willing to give up less of good 2 for another unit of good 1. This is why indifference curves are convex.

Why Diminishing MRS Happens

As you move down the curve:

  • You have more x₁ → MU₁ falls (diminishing marginal utility)
  • You have less x₂ → MU₂ rises
  • So MRS = MU₁/MU₂ falls

4 Consumer Optimization

The consumer maximizes utility subject to a budget constraint.

max U(C₁, C₂)   s.t.   P₁C₁ + P₂C₂ ≤ W

Optimality Condition

MU₁ / P₁ = MU₂ / P₂ Equal marginal utility per dollar across all goods

Equivalently:

MRS = P₁ / P₂ Indifference curve tangent to budget line

Intuition

If MU₁/P₁ > MU₂/P₂, the last dollar on good 1 gives more utility than on good 2. Buy more of good 1, less of good 2. As you do, MU₁ falls and MU₂ rises until they equalize.

Dollar-by-Dollar Allocation (Discrete Case)

  1. Compute MU per dollar for each good: MU/P
  2. Spend each dollar on the good with the highest MU/P
  3. Stop when budget is exhausted or MU/P equalizes
Linda's Example
Handbags $1, Trips $2, Income $9.
MU/$ for 1st bag = 16, 1st trip = 14, 2nd bag = 12, 2nd trip = 11, 3rd bag = 9, 3rd trip = 9.
Optimal: 3 bags + 3 trips → total utility = 37 + 68 = 105

5 Income vs Substitution Effects

When the price of a good changes, the total change in quantity demanded decomposes into two effects.

Substitution Effect

The good becomes relatively cheaper, so consumers substitute toward it.

Always positive (price down → quantity up)

Income Effect

The price change alters real purchasing power.

  • Normal good: positive
  • Inferior good: negative

Slutsky Identity

ΔC₁ = [C(P₁', W') − C(P₁, W)] + [C(P₁', W) − C(P₁', W')] Substitution effect + Income effect
Giffen Good: If the income effect dominates the substitution effect and is negative, a price decrease can lead to a decrease in quantity demanded. This violates the law of demand.

Real-World Giffen Behavior

Jensen & Miller (2008): a 1% decrease in rice prices led to a 0.24% decrease in rice consumption in poor Chinese households. Rice was a staple; when its price fell, households could afford meat, so they ate less rice.

6 Law of Demand

Law of Demand: Quantity demanded falls when price rises, other things constant.

This follows from optimization: if a good is normal, the substitution effect (negative) dominates or reinforces the income effect, so price and quantity move in opposite directions.

8 Price Elasticity of Demand

εD = − (%ΔQ) / (%ΔP) = − (∂Q/∂P) · (P/Q) Percentage change in quantity for a 1% change in price

Categories

ValueNameMeaning
|ε| > 1ElasticQuantity responds more than proportionally
|ε| = 1Unit elasticProportional response
|ε| < 1InelasticQuantity responds less than proportionally
|ε| = ∞Perfectly elasticHorizontal demand
|ε| = 0Perfectly inelasticVertical demand

Determinants of Elasticity

  • Substitutes: More substitutes → more elastic
  • Time horizon: Longer run → more elastic
  • Necessity: Less necessary → more elastic
  • Market definition: Narrower definition → more elastic
  • Budget share: Larger share → more elastic
